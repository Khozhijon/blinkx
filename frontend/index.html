    What am I looking at?

    Real pictures from space

    SPHEREx is a NASA space telescope that photographs the entire sky about every six months, in 102 colours of infrared light that our eyes can’t see. Every picture here is a small square cut out of a real SPHEREx exposure. North is up, east is left, and the date on each picture is read straight from the image file.

    Why blink?

    Distant stars and galaxies stay put. Things that are close to us — asteroids, comets, the nearest stars — drift across them. Flip quickly between two pictures of the same spot and whatever moved seems to jump. Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto this way in 1930, with two glass photographs and a machine called a blink comparator.

    Four ways to compare

    Blink flips between an earlier picture (A) and a later one (B). Side by side shows both at once. Difference subtracts A from B: anything new glows orange, anything that left turns blue, and everything that stayed the same fades to black. Time-lapse plays every exposure in order.

    Things that can fool you

    Each exposure sees a slightly different infrared colour, so a star can look brighter in one picture than another. Very bright stars leave rings and blocky cores in the difference view. Pixels that NASA’s pipeline flagged as bad (cosmic-ray hits, dead pixels) are filled in from their neighbours — tick “Show repaired pixels” to see which.
