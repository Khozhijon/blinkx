    What am I looking at?

    Real pictures from space

    SPHEREx is a NASA space telescope that photographs the entire sky about every six months, in 102 colours of infrared light that our eyes can’t see. Every picture here is a small square cut out of a real SPHEREx exposure. North is up, east is left, and the date on each picture is read straight from the image file.

    Why blink?

    Distant stars and galaxies stay put. Things that are close to us — asteroids, comets, the nearest stars — drift across them. Flip quickly between two pictures of the same spot and whatever moved seems to jump. Clyde Tombaugh found Pluto this way in 1930, with two glass photographs and a machine called a blink comparator.

    Five ways to compare

    Blink flips between an earlier picture (A) and a later one (B). Side by side shows both at once. Swipe lets you drag a divider between them. Difference subtracts A from B: anything new or brighter glows magenta, anything gone or dimmer turns green, and everything that stayed the same fades to black. Time-lapse plays every exposure in order.

    Sky Doodles

    Blink X also looks for changes by itself. It finds the stars in every picture, lines them up, and flags anything that moved, changed brightness, appeared or vanished. Each find becomes a doodle: a spark for an asteroid, a comet tail, an arrow for a nearby star, a pulse for a star that changes brightness, and a zigzag for a very distant world. Known objects are named; the rest are only candidates.

    Hunting for Planet X

    A far-off, undiscovered planet would barely move on its own. But SPHEREx looks at each spot from opposite sides of Earth’s orbit, six months apart, so a world 500 times farther from the Sun than Earth would appear to hop about 14 arcminutes between visits. Choose the 24′ field, blink two visits, and look for a faint dot that changes place.

    Things that can fool you

    Each exposure sees a slightly different infrared colour, so a star can look brighter in one picture than another. Very bright stars leave rings and blocky cores in the difference view. Pixels that NASA’s pipeline flagged as bad (cosmic-ray hits, dead pixels) are filled in from their neighbours — tick “Show repaired pixels” to see which.
